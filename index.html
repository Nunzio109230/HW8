INTRODUCTION

Two ideas at the heart of statistics

Probability theory describes uncertainty and the behaviour of random variables. Statistical inference uses observed data to learn about the processes that generate them. This page introduces four ways in which random variables can converge and explains how Bayesian inference updates probabilities when new evidence becomes available.

PART I · PROBABILITY THEORY

Modes of convergence

A sequence of random variables may approach a limiting random variable in different ways. The modes of convergence describe what “approach” means and how strongly the sequence behaves like its limit.

What is a sequence of random variables?

A sequence X1, X2, …, Xn, … is a collection of random variables defined on the same probability space. We say that it converges to a random variable X when, as n increases, the sequence approaches X according to a specified criterion.

The modes are not interchangeable: each one focuses on a different aspect of the approximation.

01Probability

Convergence in probability

P(|Xn − X| > ε) → 0

For every positive tolerance ε, the probability that the difference between Xn and X is larger than ε tends to zero.

Where
ε – any positive tolerance.
P(·) – probability of the event inside the parentheses.

Intuition: large errors become increasingly unlikely, even though individual outcomes do not have to settle down along every sample path.

A closer look: choose an acceptable error, such as ε = 0.1. We look at how often Xn differs from X by more than 0.1. As n grows, that probability must get closer to zero. This does not rule out occasional large errors; it says that they become less and less likely.

Role: this is the type of convergence stated by the Weak Law of Large Numbers.

02Almost surely

Almost sure convergence

P(limn→∞ Xn = X) = 1

The sequence converges to X for all sample outcomes except possibly a set with probability zero.

Where
lim Xn – limit of the sequence as n grows.
Probability 1 – the event occurs almost surely.

Intuition: with probability one, if we follow a single infinite sequence of outcomes, its values eventually get arbitrarily close to the limit.

A closer look: imagine generating one outcome after another and tracing the resulting sequence. Almost every possible infinite trace eventually stays as close to X as we want. Unlike convergence in probability, this statement concerns the long-run behaviour of individual sample paths, not only the probability of an error at each n.

Role: this is the type of convergence stated by the Strong Law of Large Numbers.

03L²

Mean-square convergence

E[(Xn − X)2] → 0

The expected squared difference between Xn and X tends to zero. It measures the size of the error using its squared magnitude.

Where
E[·] – expected value.
(Xn − X)² – squared approximation error.

Intuition: not only do large errors become unlikely; the average squared error itself becomes small.

A closer look: first, the error is squared, so an error twice as large contributes four times as much. Then we take its expected value, which averages this squared error over all possible outcomes. For the expectation to approach zero, the estimator must become accurate on average, with large deviations strongly penalized.

Role: useful in estimation and approximation. For an estimator θ̂n of an unknown parameter θ, the quantity E[(θ̂n − θ)2] is its mean squared error (MSE). Therefore, L² convergence of the estimator to θ means that the MSE tends to zero, providing a strong measure of estimation accuracy and consistency.

04Distribution

Convergence in distribution

Fn(x) → F(x)

The cumulative distribution functions of Xn converge to the distribution function of X at every continuity point of F.

Where
Fn(x) – CDF of Xn.
F(x) – CDF of the limiting variable X.

Intuition: the probability distributions become closer, even if the random variables themselves do not become close on the same probability space.

A closer look: instead of comparing individual values of Xn and X, we compare their cumulative distribution functions. For a chosen threshold x, Fn(x) tells us the probability that Xn is at most x. Convergence in distribution means these probabilities approach those of the limiting distribution at its continuity points. The shape of the distribution settles down, but individual outcomes need not.

Role: this is the type of convergence used by the Central Limit Theorem.

How are the four modes related?

Some modes of convergence imply others. The implications below hold in general; the reverse implications do not hold without additional assumptions.

Almost sure convergence
↓
Convergence in probability
↓
Convergence in distribution
Mean-square (L²) convergence
↓
Convergence in probability
Important: mean-square convergence and almost sure convergence are not generally comparable: neither one implies the other in all cases. Also, convergence in distribution is the weakest of these four modes. It does not generally imply convergence in probability.

The Law of Large Numbers and the Central Limit Theorem

These two fundamental results explain different aspects of the behaviour of sample averages. Suppose X1, …, Xn are independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) random variables with mean μ and finite variance σ². Their sample mean is:

X̄n = (X1 + X2 + ··· + Xn) / n
LLN · Law of Large Numbers

Where does the average go?

X̄n → μ

The sample mean approaches the population mean as the sample size increases.

  • Weak LLN: convergence in probability.
  • Strong LLN: almost sure convergence.

The precise assumptions depend on the version of the theorem. For i.i.d. variables with a finite first absolute moment, the strong LLN applies.

CLT · Central Limit Theorem

How does the average fluctuate?

√n (X̄n − μ) / σ →d N(0,1)

After centering and scaling, the sample mean approaches a standard normal distribution.

  • The convergence is in distribution.
  • It describes the shape and scale of sampling fluctuations.

This classical version assumes i.i.d. observations with finite, positive variance.

LLN vs CLT: the LLN tells us that the sample mean gets close to the true mean. The CLT tells us approximately how the sample mean is distributed around that true mean for large samples.

Why does √n appear so often?

The square root of the sample size appears because variances add under independence, while a sample mean divides a sum by n.

1 · Sum
Var(Sn) = nσ²

For independent observations with the same variance, the variance of their sum is the sum of their variances.

2 · Average
Var(X̄n) = σ²/n

Dividing the sum by n divides its variance by n².

3 · Standard error
SE(X̄n) = σ/√n

The standard deviation is the square root of the variance, so the typical error decreases at rate 1/√n.

Why independence matters: in general, Var(Sn) also contains covariance terms: 2Σi<j Cov(Xi, Xj). Independence makes these terms zero. With dependence, the variance may grow at a different rate.

This scaling is central to statistical inference. It appears in standard errors, confidence intervals and test statistics. For example, when σ is known, an approximate large-sample confidence interval for μ is:

X̄n ± z1−α/2 · σ/√n

Here z1−α/2 is the (1−α/2)-quantile of the standard normal distribution, and 1−α is the confidence level of the two-sided interval. In practice, σ is often unknown and is replaced by the sample standard deviation.

PART II · CONDITIONAL PROBABILITY & STATISTICAL INFERENCE

Bayesian inference

Bayesian inference combines what is known before observing data with the information contained in new observations. Bayes' theorem provides the mathematical rule for this update.

Conditional probability

Conditional probability measures the probability of an event A given that another event B has occurred. The information that B occurred changes the reference set to outcomes where B is true.

P(A | B) = P(A ∩ B) / P(B)
Where
P(A | B) – probability of A given B.
P(A ∩ B) – probability that A and B occur together.
P(B) – probability of event B, assumed greater than zero.

In statistics, conditional probability is also used to describe how likely observed data are under a particular condition or hypothesis. It is therefore a basic building block of statistical inference.

Bayes' theorem

Bayes' theorem follows from the definition of conditional probability. It reverses the conditioning: it allows us to calculate the probability of a hypothesis after observing evidence.

P(A | B) = [P(B | A) · P(A)] / P(B)
Where
P(A) – prior probability of A.
P(B | A) – likelihood of observing B if A is true.
P(B) – marginal probability of the evidence.
P(A | B) – posterior probability of A after observing B.

For a set of mutually exclusive and exhaustive hypotheses A1, …, Ak, the denominator can be calculated using the law of total probability:

P(B) = Σi=1k P(B | Ai) P(Ai)

A useful distinction: the likelihood P(B | A) is not the probability of the hypothesis A. It describes the probability of the observed evidence under A. The posterior P(A | B), instead, is the updated probability assigned to A after observing B.

Example 1 · Bayesian reasoning in biometric authentication

Imagine a restricted computer system that uses fingerprint recognition to verify a person's identity. The system compares the scanned fingerprint with the one associated with the claimed account and either accepts or rejects the match. For a simplified, illustrative high-risk scenario, suppose that:

  • 10% of the login attempts are made by the legitimate account owner (prior probability).
  • The system correctly accepts 98% of attempts made by the legitimate owner (genuine acceptance rate).
  • It incorrectly accepts 1% of attempts made by someone else (false acceptance rate).

We want to know: if the system accepts the fingerprint, what is the probability that the attempt was actually made by the legitimate account owner?

P(Owner | Accepted) =
[0.98 × 0.10] / [(0.98 × 0.10) + (0.01 × 0.90)]
91.6%

Posterior probability that an accepted attempt comes from the legitimate owner.

Under these illustrative assumptions, an accepted fingerprint match gives a 91.6% probability that the attempt comes from the legitimate owner. The result depends not only on the biometric system's performance, but also on the prior probability that an attempt is legitimate. These values are hypothetical and should not be interpreted as the measured performance of a real fingerprint system.

Example 2 · Learning the bias of a coin

Suppose we do not know whether a coin is fair. We represent the unknown probability of heads by θ. Before tossing the coin, we choose a Beta(2, 2) prior, which is symmetric around 0.5 and expresses an initial preference for values near 0.5 without fixing θ at exactly 0.5.

After observing 10 tosses, with 8 heads and 2 tails, the likelihood is proportional to θ8(1−θ)2. Combining it with the prior gives:

θ | data ∼ Beta(2 + 8, 2 + 2) = Beta(10, 4)

The posterior mean is:

E[θ | data] = 10 / (10 + 4) ≈ 0.714

The observed results shift our estimate towards a coin that is more likely to produce heads. With more observations, the data generally have a stronger influence on the posterior, provided the model is appropriate.

Applications, advantages and limitations

Advantages

  • Combines previous knowledge with new evidence.
  • Updates beliefs as additional data become available.
  • Produces direct probability statements about unknown hypotheses or parameters.
  • Can work well with limited data when reliable prior information exists.

Limitations

  • Results can depend on the choice of prior, especially with little data.
  • An incorrect likelihood model can lead to misleading conclusions.
  • Complex models may require demanding computations.
  • Prior information may be subjective or difficult to justify.

Bayesian inference is widely used in medical diagnosis, cybersecurity, machine learning, forecasting and scientific research. Its strength is the coherent updating of uncertainty; its reliability still depends on the quality of the assumptions and data.

CONCLUSION

From convergence to learning from evidence

The modes of convergence provide different ways to describe the limiting behaviour of random variables. The LLN explains why sample averages approach an expected value, while the CLT describes their fluctuations around it and explains the frequent appearance of the square-root sample size.

Bayesian inference addresses a complementary question: how should uncertainty about an unknown event or parameter change when new evidence is observed? Bayes' theorem gives a precise rule for combining prior information and likelihood to obtain a posterior probability.

Why assumptions matter

Both topics rely on assumptions about the data and the probability model. Independence and finite variance, for example, play an important role in the classical LLN and CLT. Bayesian conclusions also depend on the prior distribution and the likelihood model. If these assumptions do not reasonably describe the situation, the resulting conclusions may be misleading.

From probability theory to statistical inference

The LLN explains why sample averages become more reliable as the sample size increases. The CLT describes their fluctuations and helps us quantify uncertainty through approximate confidence intervals and statistical tests. These results provide a mathematical foundation for many practical statistical procedures.

Two complementary perspectives

Convergence theory studies how random quantities behave as the amount of data increases. Bayesian inference focuses on how uncertainty about unknown quantities changes when new evidence becomes available. Together, they show how probability theory supports both the long-run properties of statistical methods and the process of learning from data.

Together, these ideas form an important foundation for understanding probability, statistical estimation and decision-making under uncertainty.
